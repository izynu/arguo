# Date checks

Dates in an audit are evidence, not decoration. Four dates matter and they
are not interchangeable: the page's publication date, the page's update
date, the access date the original document claims (if any), and your own
access date during this audit. Confusions among these four are a regular
source of quiet errors in citations, which is exactly what an audit exists
to surface.

## The dates to collect

Per citation, record what is observable:

- Page publication date: when the cited page says it was published.
- Page update date: a "last updated", "last reviewed", or revised-on line,
  when present.
- The document's stated access date: if the document's citation carries an
  "accessed on" line, record it as the author's claim about their own
  access. It is never your access date.
- Audit retrieval timestamp: when you fetched the page in this audit.

Any of these can be unknown. Unknown is recorded as unknown, not inferred
from the URL, the copyright footer, or the file's position on the site.

## The checks

- Citation date versus page date
  Does the page as fetched support the date the document's citation
  carries? A citation dated 2024 that lands on a page showing a 2022
  update is a mismatch. So is a citation with no date pointing at a page
  whose date is far outside the document's own frame. Record the mismatch
  with both numbers.

- Observation period versus claim currency
  Currency is judged by the period the source describes, not by page
  freshness. A page updated yesterday can present data from a survey that
  closed years ago. Check the reference period in the content and compare
  it to the period the document's claim needs.

- Update versus what the author saw
  A page updated after the document was written may no longer show what
  the author cited. This is common and usually innocent. Record it: the
  passage verdict applies to the page as fetched now, and the note says
  the page may have changed since citation. If the document's quoted
  passage is no longer on the page, that combination (passage gone, page
  updated) is the classic moved-or-changed signature.

- Access-date honesty
  Your table's access date is the timestamp of your own fetch. Never copy
  the document's stated access date into your fetch record, and never
  present a fetch you did not perform as having happened.

## Undated and ambiguous pages

- Prefer a date printed in the content over metadata you infer. If only a
  copyright year is visible, record exactly that: "copyright 2021", not
  "published 2021".
- "Last reviewed" is a review date, not a publication date. Record which
  one you saw.
- If no date is observable at all, record unknown. A missing date on a
  cited page is itself a small quality finding; the re-grade's transparency
  criterion is where it lands.

## Archive snapshots

- If the document cites an archive or snapshot URL, the snapshot is the
  cited source. Verify the snapshot's own date and compare it with the
  date the document's citation implies.
- If the document cites a live URL and the live fetch fails or shows
  different content, you may consult an archive copy as an extra evidence
  line in the per-citation block. The link class stays what the live fetch
  showed: dead stays dead, moved stays moved. The snapshot can inform the
  note and the passage work; it does not overwrite the class.

## Format traps

- Day and month order: 03/04/2026 is ambiguous across conventions. Record
  it as ambiguous unless the page itself disambiguates.
- Fiscal years, crop years, academic years: a "2023" figure may cover a
  period ending in 2022 or spanning two years. The observation period, not
  the label, is what gets compared.
- Time zones and "as of" lines: an "as of" date is a data date, not a
  publication date.
- Seasons and quarters: "Q3" needs its year and its convention.

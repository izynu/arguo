# Misattribution patterns

A misattribution is a citation to the wrong source for the claim, and it is
a different defect from link rot: every link can work and the attribution
can still be wrong. This reference is the pattern catalog. For each pattern
it gives the evidence that identifies it and what to record.

## The patterns

1. Wrong source
   The claim comes from source A and the citation points to source B,
   usually a nearby or similar one: a different year of the same series, a
   different report from the same publisher, an organization confused with
   its sibling body. Evidence: the cited page exists but does not contain
   the claim, and a plausible holder of the claim is identifiable. Record
   both the mismatch and the candidate holder, as a candidate.

2. Syndicated copy cited as the original
   The document cites a republished copy of a story, a wire piece, or a
   press release as if it were the origin. Signs: boilerplate about where
   the piece was "originally published", a canonical link to another
   domain, identical text on multiple domains, a press release describing
   a study cited as the study. Record the original when identifiable and
   note that the claim's independence picture changes: three outlets
   carrying one wire story are one source.

3. Quote drift
   A quotation that has accumulated changes across republications: words
   dropped, qualifiers lost, wording modernized. Evidence: the document's
   quote matches a copy that differs from the earliest version you can
   reach. Record the earliest version you compared against and the
   differences. If you could not reach an earlier version, say so; do not
   assume drift you did not see.

4. Page moved since citation
   The link was correct when the document was written and now resolves
   somewhere else, or to a different document. This is not author error;
   it is time passing. Distinguish it from pattern 1 by evidence about
   history: an archive snapshot, a redirect chain that names the old
   location, or the same document at a new path. Record "moved since
   citation" and what the new location holds.

5. Attribution laundering
   A chain of secondary citations, each citing the previous blog or roundup
   and none reaching the primary. Evidence: the cited page itself
   attributes the fact elsewhere. For load-bearing claims, follow the trail
   one or two steps. The finding is not that intermediaries are useless;
   it is that the document cited a middleman as if it were the origin.

6. Review cited as the study
   A literature review, summary, or news write-up cited for a specific
   empirical result as if it were the primary record. Evidence: the cited
   page describes and links the study rather than reporting it. Record the
   primary when identifiable.

7. Landing page cited for a data point
   The citation goes to an organization's root or landing page while the
   figure lives in a specific report, dataset page, or attached file.
   Evidence: the fetched landing page does not contain the claim, and the
   specific holder is identifiable or strongly indicated. Record the
   candidate specific URL as a recommendation, not as a fact about where
   the claim lives.

## Telling author error from link rot

- If the current page never plausibly contained the claim, and the claim
  looks like it belongs to a different or more specific source: suspect
  author error (patterns 1, 2, 5, 6, 7).
- If the cited location once plausibly held the claim and history suggests
  it moved or changed: suspect link rot plus timing (pattern 4), which is
  recorded differently and reads less harshly in the verdict.
- If you cannot establish either: record what you observed and leave the
  cause open. An unexplained mismatch is a valid finding.

## What to record per finding

- The pattern name, from the catalog above.
- The evidence: the quote or absence you observed, and the comparison that
  identified the pattern.
- The candidate correct source, when you identified one, marked as a
  candidate.
- Whether the claim survives when attached to the correct source. A
  misattributed citation sometimes hides a true claim; the audit reports
  both facts.

## Trace budget

Misattribution work can expand forever. Budget it: one or two trace steps
for load-bearing claims, zero for peripheral ones, and record where the
trace stopped and why. Do not present a candidate source as verified
unless you fetched it in this audit and it passed the passage check.

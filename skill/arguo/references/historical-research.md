# Historical research

Historical questions are answered from records made at or near the time, and
the method's discipline is provenance: for every source, who made it, when,
for whom, and why. A document is evidence of its own making before it is
evidence of anything else.

## Source hierarchy

- Primary: documents and artifacts from the period: letters, minutes, laws,
  ledgers, newspapers of the day, photographs, speeches, census and parish
  records, diplomatic dispatches.
- Secondary: historical scholarship by later historians, building on
  primaries.
- Tertiary: encyclopedias and summaries. Useful for orientation and finding
  secondary literature, not for load-bearing claims.

Digitized collections and national library portals are the usual access
route to primaries; the repo catalog's history-archives and national-libraries
categories list major ones.

## Provenance questions per source

- Creator: who wrote or produced this, in what role?
- Date: when, and in what calendar system?
- Audience: public record, private letter, internal memo, propaganda?
- Purpose: why was it made, and what did the creator want?
- Survival and custody: how did it reach the archive that holds it, and is
  it complete or excerpted?

Answers go in the ledger limitations or notes. A source with unknown
provenance is usable only with that gap stated.

## Transcription and translation

- Prefer facsimiles over transcriptions when available. Transcriptions carry
  the editor's choices and errors.
- Compare conflicting transcriptions of the same document; differences in
  wording can matter exactly where the question does.
- Translations embed interpretation. For load-bearing passages, compare more
  than one translation or consult the original language, and record which
  translation was used.

## Calendars and dates

- Calendar systems changed at different times in different places, and
  historians date events accordingly. When a source's date differs between
  systems, record the date as stated with the system named.
- Regnal years, fiscal years, and local dating conventions need conversion,
  and the conversion basis recorded.

## Anachronism discipline

- Do not import modern categories into period actors' mouths. Record terms
  as the source uses them, with modern glosses clearly marked as glosses.
- Judgments about the past require distinguishing what period sources claim,
  what later scholarship infers, and what the report itself concludes. Keep
  those three layers visibly separate.

## Corroboration

- Single-source narratives are flagged as such. Many period events survive
  through one account; that is a limitation, not a license to state it as
  uncontested.
- Independent in the historical sense means different creators, places, and
  chains of transmission, not merely different modern books repeating one
  chronicle. Trace modern claims back to their period sources before
  counting them as corroboration.
- Silence is not evidence: absence of records says something about records,
  not always about events.

## Historiography

- Later historians disagree, and the disagreement is part of the answer.
  Note the major interpretations and their period and school, without
  pretending consensus exists where it does not.
- Distinguish the state of the record from the state of the argument: new
  archives change both.

## Checklist

- [ ] Provenance recorded for every period source.
- [ ] Facsimile or transcription quality noted.
- [ ] Translation provenance recorded.
- [ ] Calendar and dating conventions handled explicitly.
- [ ] Period terminology kept distinct from modern glosses.
- [ ] Single-source claims flagged.
- [ ] Modern scholarship distinguished from period claims.

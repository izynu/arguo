# Market research

Market questions attract the worst evidence in ordinary research: big round
numbers, incompatible definitions, and figures quoted from one analyst report
to another until their origin is forgotten. Discipline comes from anchoring
on primary corporate and statistical sources.

## Primary corporate sources

- Annual reports and audited financial statements: revenue, segments,
  employee counts, geographic splits, with the auditor's role stated.
- Regulatory filings and disclosures: use the official filing systems of the
  relevant regulator. Aggregator sites lag and paraphrase.
- Investor presentations and earnings calls: primary for what the company
  claims, not for what is independently true. Distinguish audited figures
  from management claims.
- Press releases: marketing artifacts. Fine for what the company says and
  launches; not independent evidence for market share or superiority claims.

## Fiscal year traps

- Companies define fiscal years differently: some end June, some September,
  some follow the calendar. Record the fiscal-year definition with every
  financial figure.
- Never line up companies' "2023" figures without checking that the periods
  overlap.

## Market sizing

- A market size is an estimate produced by a method. Insist on the method:
  top-down (a total scaled by shares) or bottom-up (unit counts times
  prices), and the definitions of what is counted.
- The same industry has many "market sizes" because definitions differ:
  revenue versus shipments, retail versus wholesale value, inclusion of
  services or adjacent categories, geographic coverage.
- When sources give different sizes, that is usually definitional, not
  factual. Classify per references/contradiction-analysis.md and never average
  across definitions.
- TAM, SAM, SOM are author-defined terms. Record the author's definitions
  alongside the figures.

## Analyst estimates

- Analyst and research-firm figures often have opaque methods and licensing
  limits on quoting. Treat as attributed estimates, not as facts.
- Note the publication date: analyst market figures age quickly, and old
  forecasts get quietly forgotten rather than retracted.
- Do not merge figures from different firms into one number. Report the
  range with provenance.

## Statistical anchors

- Official statistics: industry classification series, trade data, labor
  statistics, and census data anchor market claims in something checkable.
  The repo catalog's government-statistics and economics-finance categories
  list starting points.
- Trade associations: useful but advocacy-adjacent; note the interest.

## Competitive and share claims

- Market share claims need the market definition they assume. A share of a
  differently-defined market is a different number.
- Vendor-published comparisons carry conflicts of interest; require
  independent corroboration before carrying them.
- Customer counts, growth rates, and "fastest growing" claims each need
  their base and period captured per references/quantitative-research.md.

## Recording specifics

- Every financial figure: value, currency, nominal or real, fiscal period
  with start and end, segment definition, and source document with page or
  table reference.
- Every forecast: the forecaster, the date issued, the horizon, and the
  method when disclosed. A forecast is evidence about the forecaster, and
  about the expectation at that time; it is not evidence about the future.

## Checklist

- [ ] Primary filings used for load-bearing financial claims.
- [ ] Fiscal-year definitions recorded.
- [ ] Market definitions stated alongside every size or share figure.
- [ ] Analyst figures labeled as attributed estimates with dates.
- [ ] Conflicts of interest noted for vendor and association sources.
- [ ] Forecasts kept distinct from outcomes everywhere in the report.

# Source quality rubric

Every load-bearing source gets a recorded assessment. The rubric has ten
criteria. Assess the source for this question, not in the abstract: the same
document can be strong for one subquestion and weak for another.

## The ten criteria

1. Authority for the question
   - Ask: does this publisher or author have recognized standing on exactly
     this subject? A Nobel laureate is not an authority on their hobby.
   - Levels: high, medium, low, unknown.

2. Primary versus secondary
   - Ask: is this the originating record or a report on one?
   - Record: primary, secondary, tertiary, unknown. See
     references/primary-sources.md for domain definitions.

3. Methodological quality
   - Ask: for empirical work, are the methods described, appropriate, and
     applied to the claim? Sample, controls, definitions, error handling.
   - Levels: strong, adequate, weak, not applicable (for documents that make
     no empirical claim, like a statute text).

4. Recency
   - Ask: is the content current for the question's time boundary, judging by
     the observation period, not the page date?
   - Levels: current, aging, outdated, unknown. See
     references/publication-dates.md.

5. Directness
   - Ask: does this source speak to the question itself, or only to a
     neighbor of it?
   - Levels: direct, indirect, inferred.

6. Transparency
   - Ask: are methods, data, assumptions, and funding visible enough that
     another party could check the work?
   - Levels: documented, partial, opaque, unknown.

7. Conflicts of interest
   - Ask: does the publisher or author gain materially if the claim is
     believed? Funding, ownership, litigation, advocacy.
   - Levels: none known, possible, declared, unknown. Declared interests are
     better than undeclared ones; a declaration does not neutralize the
     interest.

8. Independent corroboration
   - Ask: do sources from other independence groups agree?
   - Levels: independent, syndicated, single-source, unknown.

9. Limitations
   - Ask: what does the source itself admit it cannot show? A source that
     states its limits is easier to use correctly than one that does not.
   - Record the limits you find, in the source's words where possible.

10. Publisher provenance
   - Ask: who actually publishes this, and is that who it appears to be?
     Check the registrable domain, the imprint, the about page. Prefronts,
     content farms, and hijacked journals exist specifically to look
     authoritative.

## Reputation is not proof

Domain reputation is a prior, not a verdict. Large publishers publish
errors and retractions; obscure publishers sometimes hold the primary data;
well-designed hoax pages imitate institutional styling. Use reputation to
decide where to look first and how much cross-checking to demand, never as a
substitute for reading the source and corroborating the claim.

## Recording the assessment

Use templates/source-assessment-template.md. One filled assessment per
load-bearing source. The recorded rationale matters more than the levels:
a reviewer should be able to see why each level was chosen.

## Red flags that demand extra checks

- No named author or publisher, or an identity that does not check out.
- Claims about methods without any method description.
- Numbers with no denominator, period, or source of their own.
- Certainty about contested topics with no acknowledgment of debate.
- Republished text stripped of its original attribution.
- A page that exists to sell something adjacent to the claim it makes.

## How rubric results are used

- High confidence per claim requires: strong authority, primary or
  independently corroborated, adequate methodology, direct, no unresolved
  conflicts of interest.
- Anything less lands at moderate, low, or insufficient. See
  references/uncertainty-and-limitations.md for mapping rubric outcomes to
  confidence language.
